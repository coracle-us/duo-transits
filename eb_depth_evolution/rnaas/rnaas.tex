\title{Eclipses Switching On: Rapid Inclination Change in the Eccentric A-star Binary EPIC 202126877}

\author[orcid=0009-0000-7524-4470]{Jon McBee}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, USA}
\email{jon.mcbee@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
EPIC~202126877 (TYC~1883-1721-1) is a 21.775-d eccentric binary of two A stars whose eclipses have
appeared within the last two decades. SuperWASP and KELT saw no eclipse in 2004--2008 ($<4.5\%$, $3\sigma$).
K2 caught a 6.2\% primary and no secondary in 2014. TESS measured a primary 20.6\% deep in 2021, 27.4\% in
2023 and 35.6\% in 2026, and a secondary growing from 4.4\% to 12.5\%. Two independent eclipse models give an
orbital inclination rising from $85.7^\circ$ (2014) to $88.5$--$88.8^\circ$ (2026). The rate rose from
$0.20^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2014--2021) to 0.31--0.36$^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2021--2026). The primary eclipses
probably began in 2007--2011 and could become total, about 40--45\% deep, in 2027--2031. The interval between
primary and secondary eclipses changes by up to 26\,min from one orbit to the next, so $e\cos\omega$ varies
over months. Both effects point to a close third body.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{\uat{Eclipsing binary stars}{444} --- \uat{Trinary stars}{1714} --- \uat{Orbital evolution}{1178}}

\section{Introduction}

Eclipses that appear or vanish, as in SS~Lac \citep{Torres2000}, V907~Sco \citep{Zasche2023} and HS~Hya
\citep{Davenport2021}, record nodal precession driven by a third body \citep{Borkovits2016}.

EPIC~202126877 (TIC~86491361; Gaia DR3 3383995253529149056; $G=10.95$) is an A5 Am star \citep{Shang2022}.
\citet{LaCourse2015} list one K2 eclipse without a period; \citet{IJspeert2024} find $P=21.78$\,d in TESS. A
companion note on K2 single events (J.~McBee 2026, submitted) reported the deepening from the same TESS
photometry. New here: ground limits, an inclination history, eclipse timing and predictions. ADS, SIMBAD and
VizieR list no other study.

\section{Data}

I used K2 Campaign~0 \citep[2014 May;][]{Howell2014} from EVEREST \citep{Luger2016} and K2SFF
\citep{Vanderburg2014}; TESS Sectors 43--45 (2021) and 71--72 (2023) from TESS-SPOC \citep{Caldwell2020}, QLP
\citep{Huang2020} and my TESScut \citep{Brasseur2019} photometry; 2026 January full-frame images (BTJD
4055.8--4063.0); SuperWASP (2004--2008; \citealt{Pollacco2006,Butters2010}), KELT-North
(2006--2008; \citealt{Pepper2007}) and DASCH plates (1890--1990; \citealt{Grindlay2012}). ZTF and ATLAS
saturate. A $G=14.9$ neighbor at $21''$ adds about 3\% of the TESS light. TESS depths are the median flux within
$\pm15$\,min of mid-eclipse in one fixed aperture; errors are the scatter between eclipses; the aperture changes
the absolute scale by 1--2\%.

The primary is 6.2\% deep (2014), $20.6\pm0.1\%$ (2021), $27.4\pm0.2\%$ (2023) and
35.6\% (2026), and the secondary $<0.1\%$, $4.4\pm0.1\%$ and $12.5\pm0.1\%$ (none in 2026). Their ratio, immune
to constant dilution, doubles from 0.21 to 0.46.

Folded on the TESS ephemeris, the ground data limit the 2004--2008 primary to $<4.5\%$ ($3\sigma$; typically
$<1.6\%$) for phase offsets of $-0.6$ to $+0.3$\,d. DASCH shows no excess of faint points against 30
comparison stars, excluding deep eclipses through most of the century.

\section{Inclination history}

A joint fit of all eleven TESS eclipses uses two limb-darkened spheres on a Keplerian orbit, with shared radii,
surface-brightness ratio and $e\sin\omega$, one inclination per season, a time shift per eclipse, and
$e\cos\omega$ from each season's secondary phase (2026 tied to 2023). It gives reduced
$\chi^2=1.43$, $R_1/a=0.0297$, $R_2/a=0.0391$, $e=0.35$ and $\omega\approx138^\circ$. The K2 primary fixes the
2014 inclination; the absent secondary is reproduced.

The inclination rises from $85.7^\circ$ (2014.4) to $87.3^\circ$ (2021.8), $87.9^\circ$ (2023.9) and
$88.8^\circ$ (2026.0). Third light (0--15\%) and phase assumptions shift these by $0.1$--$0.4^\circ$, exceeding
the formal errors, mostly in one direction. A blind re-analysis (independent code and photometry, varied
limb darkening) gives 85.8, 87.2, 87.9 and $88.5^\circ$.

The rate rose from $0.20^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2014--2021) to 0.31--0.36$^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2021--2026); the
analyses differ mainly on the 2026 eclipse, whose egress fell in a gap. A constant TESS-era rate, predicting a 2014
primary only about 1\% deep, is excluded.

Primary eclipses need $i>84.4^\circ$, secondaries $i>86.5^\circ$. The 2014--2021 rate puts the primary onset in
2007--2011, consistent with the ground limits; a quadratic turning around in 2010 would give a 4--7\% primary in
2004--2008, which they exclude. Secondaries began around 2018--2019. The primary becomes total (flat-bottomed,
40--45\% deep) at $i\approx89.2$--$89.9^\circ$ (the radius split is poorly constrained), in 2027--2031 at the
current rate (Figure~\ref{fig:hist}). With the Gaia distance and light ratio, the radii (1.6 and
$2.1\,R_\odot$) and total mass ($\approx4.3\,M_\odot$) suit two A stars.

\section{Timing and a third body}

TESS primary times scatter by 10\,min rms about a linear ephemeris ($\chi^2=483$, 4 degrees of freedom) in three
reductions and two timing methods. The primary-to-secondary interval fell by $26\pm2.5$\,min within one 2021
orbit, but only 3--4\,min per orbit on average over 2021--2023. Within each season primary and secondary times
move oppositely about a steady mean: a changing $e\cos\omega$ over months, not light-travel time.

Relativistic, tidal and rotational apsidal motion total $<0.01^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$, orders of magnitude too slow.
Rapid nodal precession and these timing variations require a close third body. An order-of-magnitude
precession estimate gives an outer period of $\sim$100--600\,d, the fast changes favoring the short end. Gaia DR3
\citep{Gaia2023} shows normal RUWE (1.007), no non-single-star solution and no co-moving companion, implying a
tertiary below $\sim$0.3\,$M_\odot$ or an outer period near 100\,d.
