\title{Eclipses Switching On: Rapid Inclination Change in the Eccentric A-star Binary EPIC 202126877}

\author[orcid=0009-0000-7524-4470]{Jon McBee}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, USA}
\email{jon.mcbee@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
EPIC~202126877 (TYC~1883-1721-1) is a 21.775-d eccentric binary of two A stars whose eclipses have
appeared within the last two decades. SuperWASP and KELT saw no eclipse in 2004--2008 ($<4.5\%$, $3\sigma$).
K2 caught a 6.2\% primary and no secondary in 2014. TESS measured a primary 20.6\% deep in 2021, 27.4\% in
2023 and 35.6\% in 2026, and a secondary growing from 4.4\% to 12.5\%. Two independent eclipse models give an
orbital inclination rising from $85.7^\circ$ (2014) to $88.5$--$88.8^\circ$ (2026). The rate rose from
$0.20^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2014--2021) to 0.31--0.36$^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2021--2026). The primary eclipses
probably began in 2007--2011 and could become total, about 40--45\% deep, in 2027--2031. The interval between
primary and secondary eclipses changes by up to 26\,min from one orbit to the next, so $e\cos\omega$ varies
over months. Both effects point to a close third body.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Binaries whose eclipses appear or disappear are uncommon. SS~Lac \citep{Torres2000}, V907~Sco
\citep{Lacy1999,Zasche2023} and HS~Hya \citep{Davenport2021} are well-studied cases, and Kepler and TESS have
added a few dozen more. They record nodal precession driven by a third body, and so measure the architecture
of a hierarchical triple \citep[e.g.,][]{Borkovits2016}.

EPIC~202126877 (TIC~86491361; Gaia DR3 3383995253529149056; $G=10.95$) is an Am star \citep[A5;][]{Shang2022}.
\citet{LaCourse2015} list it with a single K2 Campaign~0 eclipse and no period, and \citet{IJspeert2024}
detect it automatically in TESS with $P=21.78$\,d. A companion note on K2 single events (J.~McBee 2026,
submitted) reported the deepening of its eclipses from the same TESS photometry. Here I add pre-K2 ground
limits, a physical eclipse model that turns the depths into an inclination history, the eclipse timing, and
predictions. No other study of the system's eclipses exists (ADS full text, SIMBAD, VizieR).

\section{Data and depths}

I used:
\begin{itemize}
\item K2 Campaign~0 \citep[2014 May;][]{Howell2014}, from EVEREST \citep{Luger2016} and K2SFF
  \citep{Vanderburg2014};
\item TESS Sectors 43--45 (2021) and 71--72 (2023), from TESS-SPOC \citep{Caldwell2020}, QLP \citep{Huang2020},
  and my own TESScut \citep{Brasseur2019} photometry;
\item a 2026 January TESS full-frame segment (BTJD 4055.8--4063.0) with my own photometry;
\item ground photometry: SuperWASP (2004--2008; \citealt{Pollacco2006,Butters2010}), KELT-North (2006--2008;
  \citealt{Pepper2007}) and DASCH plates \citep[1890--1990;][]{Grindlay2009}.
\end{itemize}
ZTF and ATLAS saturate on this star. The faint neighbor ($G=14.9$ at $21''$) contributes about 3\% of the
light in TESS. For TESS, all depths come from one fixed aperture, with the depth taken as the median flux
within $\pm15$\,min of mid-eclipse. The quoted errors are the scatter between eclipses; the absolute scale
depends on the aperture by 1--2\%.

The depths repeat those of the companion note. The primary is 6.2\% deep (2014), $20.6\pm0.1\%$ (2021),
$27.4\pm0.2\%$ (2023) and 35.6\% (2026). The secondary is $<0.1\%$ (2014), $4.4\pm0.1\%$ (2021) and
$12.5\pm0.1\%$ (2023); none fell in the 2026 segment. The secondary-to-primary ratio, which no constant
dilution can alter, doubles from 0.21 to 0.46.

The ground data are new. Folded on the TESS ephemeris, they limit the primary to below 4.5\% ($3\sigma$,
typically $<1.6\%$) in 2004--2008, for any phase offset from $-0.6$ to $+0.3$\,d. DASCH shows no excess of
faint points relative to 30 comparison stars, which rules out deep eclipses lasting through most of the
century.

\section{Inclination history}

I fit all eleven TESS eclipses jointly with two limb-darkened spheres on a Keplerian orbit.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Free parameters:} the radii, the surface-brightness ratio and $e\sin\omega$, shared across
  seasons; one inclination per season; and a time shift per eclipse. $e\cos\omega$ is set by each season's
  secondary phase; for 2026, which has no secondary, it is tied to 2023.
\item \textbf{Fit:} reduced $\chi^2=1.43$, $R_1/a=0.0297$, $R_2/a=0.0391$, $e=0.35$ and $\omega\approx138^\circ$.
\item \textbf{2014:} the K2 primary then fixes the inclination, and the model reproduces the absent secondary.
\end{itemize}
The inclination rises from $85.7^\circ$ (2014.4) to $87.3^\circ$ (2021.8), $87.9^\circ$ (2023.9) and
$88.8^\circ$ (2026.0). Across third light (0--15\%) and phase assumptions the values move by
$0.1$--$0.4^\circ$, larger than the formal errors, mostly in the same direction for all seasons. A blind
re-analysis with independent code, its own photometry and varied limb darkening agrees within these ranges:
85.8, 87.2, 87.9 and $88.5^\circ$.

The rate rose from $0.20^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2014--2021) to 0.31--0.36$^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$ (2021--2026). The two
analyses differ mainly on the single 2026 eclipse, whose egress falls in a data gap. A constant rate is
excluded: the TESS-era rate would predict a 2014 primary only about 1\% deep. The data cannot tell whether the
rate is still increasing.

Primary eclipses require $i>84.4^\circ$ and secondaries $i>86.5^\circ$. Extrapolating the 2014--2021 rate back
from 2014 places the primary onset in about 2007--2011, consistent with the ground limits. A quadratic trend
through the four epochs, turning around in 2010, would predict a 4--7\% primary in 2004--2008, which those
limits exclude. The secondary onset falls in about 2018--2019. The primary becomes total (flat-bottomed, about
40--45\% deep) at $i\approx89.2$--$89.9^\circ$; the split between the two radii is poorly constrained. At the
current rate that happens in 2027--2031 (Figure~\ref{fig:hist}). With the Gaia distance and the fitted light
ratio, the radii ($1.6$ and $2.1\,R_\odot$) and total mass ($\approx4.3\,M_\odot$) are consistent with two A
stars.

\section{Eclipse timing and a third body}

The TESS primary times scatter by 10\,min rms about a linear ephemeris ($\chi^2=483$ for 4 degrees of
freedom), consistently across three reductions and two timing methods. The interval from primary to
secondary eclipse fell by $26\pm2.5$\,min within one orbit in 2021, but by only 3--4\,min per orbit on average
between 2021 and 2023. Within each season the primary and secondary times move in opposite directions, while
their mean stays on the period. That pattern is a changing $e\cos\omega$, not light-travel time, and it
varies over months.

General-relativistic, tidal and rotational apsidal motion together are below $0.01^\circ$\,yr$^{-1}$, orders of
magnitude short of the observed change in $\omega$. Rapid nodal precession together with these dynamical
timing variations therefore requires a close third body. An order-of-magnitude precession estimate gives an
outer period of $\sim$100--600\,d, and the fast orbit-to-orbit changes favor the short end. Gaia DR3
\citep{Gaia2023} shows a normal RUWE (1.007), no non-single-star solution and no co-moving wide companion. That
points to a tertiary below $\sim$0.3\,$M_\odot$ or an outer period near 100\,d.

\section{Predictions}

On the linear TESS ephemeris ($P=21.774799$\,d, $T_0={\rm BJD_{TDB}}\,2459488.58926$), the next primaries
fall on 2026 Oct~25 22:39, Nov~16 17:12, Dec~8 11:46 and Dec~30 06:21~UTC ($\pm30$\,min, set by the timing
variations). Each lasts about 8\,h at $V\approx11$, which is within reach of small telescopes. Timings and
depths through 2027--2031 will test the predicted totality and whether the rate keeps increasing. Radial
velocities and a photodynamical model could then measure the tertiary's orbit and mass. Data, code and fits are
at \url{https://github.com/coracle-us/duo-transits} (directory \texttt{eb\_depth\_evolution}).
