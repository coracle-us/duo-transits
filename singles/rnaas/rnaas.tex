\title{Revisiting Stale TESS Single-transit Community Candidates: Mostly Eclipsing Binaries, Two Candidates with a Second Transit}

\author[orcid=0009-0000-7524-4470]{Jon McBee}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, USA}
\email{jon.mcbee@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
Community TESS Objects of Interest (CTOIs) flagged as single transits are rarely revisited.
I re-examined 303 such stars against all TESS data through Sector 108, with a validated repeat
search, difference imaging and Gaia DR3 binarity diagnostics. I recover the catalogued event for 272 stars; most of the other 31 have a wrong
catalogued epoch or no significant dip. Of the 272, 79 show repeats: 21 are Gaia-flagged binaries,
18 are blends, 23 fit no single period, and 17 needed vetting by hand. A search for dips of any depth
shows that the CTOI 147035057.01 event is the secondary eclipse of a 37.315\,d eccentric binary
with a 5.5\% primary. Two on-target candidates with two transits survive: TIC 159614322, where a
partial third transit leaves $P=89.965$ or $179.930$\,d, and TIC 229055790. For 193 candidates that
remain single, a median 52\% of log-period space (5--1500\,d) is now excluded.
\end{abstract}

\section{Sample}

ExoFOP lists hundreds of CTOIs uploaded as single transits, with no period, mostly in
2020--2022. Of 794 open CTOIs with no period or tagged ``single'', ``duo'' or ``mono'', I excluded
the 344 uploaded by groups still following up their own single transits, identified by the ExoFOP
uploader: members of the TESS Single Transit Planet Candidate working group (223), the NGTS duotransit program
\citep[55;][]{Hawthorn2024}, and the transformer search of \citet{Salinas2025} (66). From the remaining
450 I then removed 46 with missing parameters, 8 on stars
hosting confirmed planets, 4 on stars with a TOI, and 24 on catalogued TESS eclipsing binaries
\citep{Prsa2022}. This left 368 CTOIs on 359 stars. Of these, 32 already carry a community
period and were not re-analyzed. Another 22 fall outside the range I analyzed: 7 are brighter than
$T=7$, and 15 have catalogued depths outside 200--50{,}000\,ppm, mostly placeholder values near
100\%. I analyzed the remaining 305 stars, plus one pilot target (TIC 53847727) later found in the
TESS EB catalogue. Three of these CTOIs carry truncated TIC IDs that duplicate other CTOIs in the
sample (e.g., TIC 1461133 for 14611330), leaving 303 unique stars.

\section{Methods}

I used SPOC 2-min, TESS-SPOC \citep{Caldwell2020} and QLP \citep{Huang2020} light curves from
MAST. For Sectors $\geq100$ without pipeline products, I performed my own aperture photometry
on TESScut cutouts \citep{Brasseur2019}. I tested it against SPOC 2-min light curves of ten stars
($T=8.5$--14.1, Sectors 33 and 91--107), eight of them hosts of known transiting planets. Its 8--9
pixel aperture collects 0.78--0.99 of the SPOC SAP flux for $T<11.5$, and more for fainter stars,
where SPOC uses 4--7 pixels. Transit depths match PDCSAP to 3\% for
uncrowded hosts, but are about 30\% shallower for crowded ones, since I apply no crowding
correction. Mid-times agree to 2\,min, and the per-cadence scatter is a median 1.07 times SPOC's.
The main difference is flux steps of a few ppt between data segments.

I first test whether the catalogued event is present: a dip deeper than $0.5\,\delta$ at SNR
$\geq5$ within $\pm0.03$\,d of the catalogued epoch, where $\delta$ and $D$ are the catalogued depth
and duration. The repeat search then slides a box of that depth and duration on a $D/4$ grid,
with segment-aware local baselines. A detection needs SNR $\geq8$, a depth of 0.5--2$\,\delta$, at
least 80\% coverage, and baseline on both sides. This matching is deliberate: only a recurrence
of the same event can enter $P=\Delta T/n$, while an eclipse of different depth (e.g., a
secondary at unknown phase) or a second body cannot. Candidate periods of 5--1500\,d come from
linear-ephemeris fits to all events. Any period with a predicted epoch on flat data is
rejected. I validated the method blind, starting from the first transit only, on 26 TESS
stars with two transits and a known period, including duotransit candidates of \citet{Hawthorn2024}. It kept the true period
in 26/26 and returned it as the unique solution in 19, with no wrong unique answers.

I localized sources with TESScut difference images, measuring offsets from the target's Gaia
DR3 position \citep{Gaia2023}. A source is off-target if it is offset by $\geq6''$ at
$\geq3\sigma$; ``firm'' requires two agreeing images. Binarity indicators are Gaia DR3 \texttt{non\_single\_star}, two-body orbits, and RV
variability \citep[$p\leq0.01$, renormalised GOF $>4$;][]{Katz2023}.

To find what the matched search misses, I re-searched all 272 stars for dips of any depth
(durations 1--24\,h). The cuts were SNR $\geq8$ including red noise, at least 80\% coverage,
baseline on both sides, no edge or momentum-dump coincidence, and confirmation in a second
reduction. The identical search on inverted light curves gives 0.085 false events per
star-sector, against 0.092 for the real data. As a population, extra dips below SNR $\approx20$
are therefore no more common than noise, so I accept individual extra events only after vetting
by eye. The search re-detects 234 of the 272 catalogued events; most misses fall below its SNR
cut. It recovers the secondary eclipses of six known TESS EBs.

\section{Results}

The event test used pipeline light curves at every catalogued epoch (SPOC 2-min for 285 stars),
never my own photometry. Of the 303 stars, 31 fail it. I inspected each in every available light
curve: SPOC SAP and PDCSAP, TESS-SPOC, QLP, six other reductions, and my own photometry. None
fails because of the photometry used. In three near-threshold cases, a different pipeline product
would pass the test. Eighteen of the 31 come from one uploader's batch, including all six
half-day epoch slips.
\begin{itemize}
\item 9 CTOIs have wrong epochs; six are 0.5\,d early.
\item 13 show only a dip shallower than $0.5\,\delta$ or below SNR 5.
\item 6 show no dip in any reduction.
\item 3 are explained by flagged data, a blend with a neighboring CTOI, or no data at the epoch.
\end{itemize}
Of the 272 stars with the catalogued event, 193 show no repeat. The 79 stars with repeats break
down as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item 21 are on Gaia-flagged binaries. Three Gaia SB1 orbits reproduce the TESS periods
  (Table~\ref{tab:systems}), so these are eccentric eclipsing binaries.
\item 18 are firmly off-target, i.e.\ blends with neighbors.
\item 23 show repeats that no single period of 5--1500\,d explains.
\item 17 were vetted by hand: 2 survive (below), 3 remain tentative, 5 are possibly off-target,
  and 7 were rejected (inconsistent depths, absent in an independent reduction, or a host too
  large).
\end{itemize}
The 3 tentative repeats are seen only in my own Sector~107 photometry, which has inter-segment steps.

In the any-depth search, vetted extra eclipses appear on 14 stars: nine already classed as
binaries, four of the 23 without a single period, and one single, TIC 250492521, whose differently
shaped event (in three reductions) makes it an EB or blend candidate. No single star gains a period.
The extra eclipses give periods for four stars, including TIC 188835620 (22.217\,d) and TIC 83384234
(4.33\,d) from the no-single-period class. For two, the catalogued event is the secondary of a
deeper eclipse.

\textit{CTOI 147035057.01.} Three catalogued events (Sectors 7, 33, and 108, the last from my own
photometry) fix $P=37.31497\pm0.00003$\,d; the S108 event rules out every shorter alias. The
any-depth search shows that they are the secondary eclipses of a binary. A V-shaped primary eclipse
5.3--5.6\% deep falls at phase 0.239 relative to them, in Sector 6 (TESS-SPOC) and Sector 87 (QLP),
exactly 59 cycles apart. All Gaia sources within $60''$ together supply only 3.5\% of the target's
flux, so the deep eclipse must be on the target. Gaia DR3 measures an RV amplitude of
50\,km\,s$^{-1}$, so the companion is stellar. The depth ratio argues against near-equal twins,
but grazing geometry in the eccentric orbit ($e\cos\omega\approx0.4$) could produce it, and Gaia DR4
epoch RVs should decide.

\textit{TIC 159614322.} Transits in Sectors 14 and 41 have consistent depths ($\approx3.2$\,ppt,
$\approx9.7$\,h) across products, and they are on target. Flat data leave $P=719.72/n$ with
$n\in\{1,2,4,5,8\}$. Sector 21 then contains an ingress and $\approx9$\,h of transit floor,
with the egress lost in the orbit-1 downlink gap. It appears in TESS-SPOC, QLP, and my own
photometry, with a flat background and no pointing step. A blind re-analysis with independent
code also found it. It selects $n=8$ or $n=4$, giving $P=89.9651\pm0.0006$ or
$179.9302\pm0.0012$\,d with $T_0={\rm BJD_{TDB}}\,2458703.8277\pm0.0041$. These values are fitted
from the two full transits; the partial transit arrives 13--26\,min late, possibly a bias from
the missing egress. The companion is $\approx0.9\,R_{\rm J}$, and the host is
RV-stable in Gaia DR3. Both periods predict a transit at 2026-12-28 06:40\,UTC ($\pm45$\,min,
errors inflated $\times2.1$). A sweep of all 272 stars for gap-truncated
transits found no other convincing case.

\textit{TIC 229055790.} This candidate, uploaded by the Planet Hunters TESS project
\citep[see][]{Eisner2021}, shows transits in
Sectors 1 and 68 with consistent depths, on target, re-detected independently. With 38 allowed
periods $\geq26.7$\,d and $R_p\approx3.8\,R_\oplus$, it is worth monitoring.

\section{Conclusion}

Stale single-transit CTOIs with repeat signals are mostly stellar, and inexpensive Gaia binarity
checks and difference imaging dispose of most of them. The residue is small but useful: two
on-target candidates with two transits and alias lists short enough to target, and a few tentative repeats that
pipeline products will confirm or reject. Code, light-curve products and classifications are at
\url{https://github.com/coracle-us/duo-transits}.
