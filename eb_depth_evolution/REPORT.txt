0. ONE-PARAGRAPH SUMMARY
------------------------
A detached, eccentric (e = 0.35) A-type (Am) binary with P = 21.775 d whose eclipses are switching on. The primary eclipse
depth went from 6.2% (K2, May 2014) to 20.6% (TESS, late 2021), 27.4% (late 2023) and 35.6% (TESS, 15 Jan 2026). The secondary
was absent in 2014 (< 0.03%), 4.4% in 2021 and 12.5% in 2023 (secondary/primary ratio 0.21 -> 0.46, dilution-independent).
SuperWASP (2004-2008) and KELT-North (2006-2008) show no eclipse: primary < 4.5% (3 sigma, worst case; typically < 1.6%) for
any time offset in -0.6..+0.3 d around the predicted ephemeris. A joint physical eclipse model gives an orbital inclination
rising from 85.7 deg (2014) to 87.3 (2021), 87.9 (2023) and 88.8 deg (2026), about 0.25 deg/yr on average and accelerating
(0.21 -> 0.31 -> 0.40 deg/yr). The primary eclipse probably started between about 2008 and 2011, and the secondary
around 2018-2019. The primary should become total (flat-bottomed, about 40% deep) once i > 89.25 deg, around 2027-2029.
Timing shows ETVs of 10-20 min from one orbit to the next, with primary and secondary moving in opposite directions, and a
secular drift of the secondary phase (0.3322 -> 0.3282 in 2 yr). These require e*cos(w) to change on timescales of months,
which only a dynamically close third body can do. Gaia DR3 astrometry is normal (RUWE 1.007), there is no NSS solution, and
there are no RVs, so the tertiary is inferred, not seen.

3. ECLIPSE TIMING
-----------------
Times (data/eclipse_times.csv) are cross-checked three ways: SPOC vs QLP vs own photometry agree within 0.5 min, and a
mirror-symmetry (Kwee-van Woerden-style) method agrees within 0.7 min (14_timing_check.py). The exception is the partial
S72 secondary (egress only), whose two time estimates differ by 15 min; it is excluded.
 - TESS primaries, linear: P = 21.774799 +- 0.000013 d (formal), T0 = 2488.58926. chi2 = 483 for 4 dof, O-C rms 10.3 min.
   O-C (min): E0 -7.8, E1 +5.5, E2 +11.5, E35 -18.2, E36 +1.8, E72 +8.9. These are real: three independent reductions
   agree.
 - K2 primary (E = -124): -102 min relative to that ephemeris. Inflating the ephemeris error by sqrt(chi2/dof) = 11
   gives 30 min, so the offset is 3.4 sigma. With the 2026 eclipse included it is 1.7 h, not the 2.2 h in the K2 note.
 - Quadratic fit over all primaries with a fitted jitter: jitter 14 min, c2 = -2.7e-6 +- 1.1e-6 d (2.6 sigma). This is not
   a meaningful detection of dP/dt given the jitter.
 - Secondary minus primary, the key dilution- and LTTE-independent quantity (21_timing_models.py):
     2021: 7.24283 d (E0), then 7.22460 d (E1): -26.2 +- 2.5 min in ONE orbit (confirmed by SPOC and KvW times).
     2023: 7.17192 (E34, using an estimated primary, +-15 min), then 7.15087 d (E35).
     Mean change 2021 -> 2023: -104 min, or -3.1 min/orbit.
   So e*cos(w) drops by about 0.0013 in a single orbit in late 2021, while the two-year average rate is ten times
   smaller. e*cos(w) must therefore oscillate on a timescale of months, with an amplitude in T_S - T_P of order an
   hour or more. Within each season the primary and secondary O-C move in opposite directions: (P+S)/2 stays on the mean
   period within 0.2 min. That is the signature of a varying e*cos(w) (dynamical apsidal motion), not of light travel time.
   Tidal and GR apsidal motion are negligible: GR is about 0.003 deg/yr, and the classical term is far smaller at
   R/a ~ 0.03-0.04. So the timing alone requires a dynamically important third body.
 - Models NOT fitted: there are 6 primary and 4 secondary times. That is too few for an LTTE or dynamical orbit, and any
   such fit would be over-interpreted. A photodynamical model (TESS light curves plus times) is the right next step and
   needs more epochs.
 - No extra (tertiary) eclipses were found in any TESS sector (15_extra_events.py). A 1% dip in S44 at BTJD 2509.2
   scales with aperture size and coincides with a background bump, so it is scattered light.
 - Predicted primaries, 2026-27 season (TESS linear ephemeris; allow +-30 min for ETVs): 2026-10-25 22:43, 11-16 17:19,
   12-08 11:55, 12-30 06:31, 2027-01-21 01:06, 02-11 19:42, 03-05 14:18 UTC. Eclipse duration about 7 h, V = 11:
   easy targets for amateurs.

4. PHYSICAL PICTURE
-------------------
Joint physical fit (16_physical_fit.py; model in ebmodel.py: two limb-darkened spheres on a Kepler orbit, validated
against batman to 1e-5). Inputs: all 11 TESS eclipses. Free parameters: r1 = R1/a, r2 = R2/a, surface-brightness ratio J,
e*sin(w), one inclination per season, and a free conjunction time per event. e*cos(w) per season is fixed from the
measured secondary phase. Result: red. chi2 = 1.43; the residual plot is figures/physical_fit_events_base.png.
  r1 = 0.0297, r2 = 0.0391 (R2/R1 = 1.32), J = 0.877, e*sin(w) = 0.231, e = 0.348 (2021) -> 0.352 (2023), w = 138.3 -> 139.0 deg
  i: 2014.36  85.69-85.84 (K2, Kepler-band LD; range over the assumed 2014 secondary phase and the systematic variants)
     2021.81  87.24-87.39
     2023.87  87.88-88.10
     2026.04  88.72-89.08
  Systematic variants: third light 0, 0.02 or 0.15; 2026 e*cos(w) assumption; 2014 phase 0.32-0.35. The formal errors
  are about 0.003 deg, so the systematics dominate. The model also correctly predicts NO secondary in 2014 (i < 86.55).
  Primary eclipses exist for i > 84.47 deg, secondaries for i > 86.55. Totality (primary) needs i > 89.25.
  Maximum depths (TESS band): primary 39.7% (total), secondary 37.6%.
Rates (18_inclination_trend.py, data/inclination_trend.csv): di/dt = 0.19-0.22 deg/yr (2014-2021), 0.31-0.34 (2021-2023),
  0.39-0.45 (2023-2026); linear average 0.25 deg/yr. Strictly, photometry measures |cos i| decreasing; the system is
  heading toward edge-on.
Onset:
  - Primary: linear i(t) gives onset 2009.2-2010.6. A quadratic (turnaround) i(t) predicts a 4-7% primary in 2004-2008,
    which the WASP+KELT limits exclude (< 4.5% worst case, typically < 1.6%, within -0.6..+0.3 d). So the primary
    eclipses most likely began between about 2008 and 2011, certainly between 2008 and 2014.
  - Secondary: 2017.7-2018.2 (linear) or 2019.1-2019.3 (quadratic). ASAS-SN g-band could test this directly.
Future: i = 90 is reached around 2028-2032. The primary becomes total around 2027-2029, then (if the node keeps
  precessing) the eclipses shrink again. The primary would vanish once i passes 95.5, which at 0.25-0.45 deg/yr is the
  2040s-2050s. This is very rough.
Sanity check on the stars: Gaia distance 661 pc, A_G = 0.37 and M_G(system) = 1.49. With the fitted light ratio
  (L2/L1 = 1.52 in TESS) and Teff about 7400-7700 K, this gives R1 = 1.6 and R2 = 2.1 Rsun, so a = 53 Rsun and
  M_tot = 4.3 Msun. That is consistent with two A stars (expected about 3.9 Msun). The geometry is self-consistent.
Third body:
  - Nodal precession at 0.2-0.45 deg/yr, together with e*cos(w) varying over months, points to a compact hierarchical
    triple. The order-of-magnitude estimate T_prec ~ (4/3)(M/m3)(P2^2/P1) gives P2 of roughly 100-600 d for
    m3/M = 0.05-0.3 and mutual inclination 2-10 deg. Stability requires P2 >~ 100 d.
  - Gaia DR3: RUWE 1.007 and AEN 0.118 mas, at the 55th and 42nd percentiles of 177 similar stars within 2 deg.
    No NSS solution, no epoch RVs. A 0.5 Msun tertiary at P2 = 200 d would move the photocentre by about 0.2 mas and
    should raise RUWE noticeably. So Gaia mildly prefers a low-mass tertiary (<~0.2-0.3 Msun) or an unfavourable
    geometry. This is a rough argument, not a measurement.
  - No co-moving wide companion within 10' (plx 1.2-1.85, |dPM| < 1.5 mas/yr); only field stars of G = 16.8-20.5.
  - LAMOST: one LRS spectrum (2017-12-07; A5, Am; RV 5.2 +- 1.6 km/s, a single epoch, so no constraint). The MRS
    spectra (2018-11-21, 2020-11-08) have S/N 0-3, an apparent fibre problem, and their "RVs" scatter by hundreds of
    km/s, so they are useless. No row in J/ApJS/258/26 (that catalogue needs S/N > 5).

5. VERDICT
----------
Publishable as an RNAAS: YES, but with three conditions.
What is genuinely new:
 (1) A quantitative inclination history (85.7 -> 88.8 deg, 2014-2026, accelerating 0.2 -> 0.4 deg/yr) from a physical model
     that also reproduces the 2014 non-detection of the secondary.
 (2) Pre-K2 ground photometry (WASP, KELT; DASCH for the century) shows the eclipses are recent: primary onset about
     2008-2011, secondary about 2018-19.
 (3) Timing evidence that e*cos(w) is changing on month timescales (secondary-minus-primary interval -26 min in one orbit,
     -104 min over two years). This independently implies a close third body.
 (4) Testable predictions: total primary eclipses at about 40% depth by about 2027-29; dates for the next eclipses.
 There are only about a dozen known binaries caught switching eclipses on or off (e.g. SS Lac, HS Hya, V907 Sco, and
 the few TESS/Kepler cases). One with K2+TESS precision, an eccentric orbit and fast dynamical ETVs is a good find.
 One figure (figures/depth_history_summary.png) fits the RNAAS format.
What is shaky:
 - Only four inclination epochs. The model holds e, R1/a, R2/a and J fixed in time, and third light is unknown
   (0-15% tested). Absolute inclinations shift by up to 0.3 deg between variants; the trend is robust.
 - Onset dates are extrapolations, and the ground limits depend on the ephemeris offset assumed for 2004-08 (-0.6..+0.3 d
   covered; the true offset could be larger if the ETVs are large).
 - The third body is inferred, not detected; no RVs; the Gaia constraint is qualitative. The timing interpretation is
   qualitative: there are too few times to fit an orbit.
 - "Sector 1751" is a TESScut product label (known MAST oddity). Its photometry is internally consistent (depth 35.1-35.7%
   for r = 1.0-2.0 px), but it is one eclipse with the egress truncated.
 - Overlap with our own pending K2 note (AAS82225), which already announces the deepening. A second note must cite it and
   stress what is new (inclination, onset, timing, predictions). The K2 note's "32% in 2025" should be corrected to
   "36% in January 2026" at proof stage.
Before submitting: (i) run an ADS full-text search; (ii) Jon fetches the ASAS-SN Sky Patrol light curve by hand (reCAPTCHA)
 to fill 2014-2021 and test the 2018-19 secondary onset; (iii) ideally get one or two ground-based eclipse timings in
 the 2026-27 season (dates in section 3). A stronger, later paper (AJ/MNRAS) would need RVs plus a photodynamical model;
 Borkovits, Rappaport or Kostov would likely be interested.
