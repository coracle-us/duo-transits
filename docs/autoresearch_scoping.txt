AUTORESEARCH SCOPING: which problem should an overnight agent loop attack?
Written 2026-10-12 for Jon McBee (duo-transits repo). Research only; no code changes made.
Hardware assumed: RTX A4500 (20 GB), 32 CPU cores, ~100 experiments x ~5 min per night.

Confidence tags:
  [V]  = checked this session against the source (arXiv abstract or HTML, GitHub, Zenodo, or a local probe)
  [R]  = from memory or secondary summaries; verify before citing

======================================================================
0. WHAT MAKES A GOOD AUTORESEARCH TARGET (the bar used below)
======================================================================
karpathy/autoresearch [V] (https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch):
  - the agent edits only train.py; prepare.py (data and evaluation) is frozen;
  - the metric is val_bpb, a single scalar;
  - each run gets a fixed 5-minute wall-clock budget, about 100 runs overnight;
  - keep a change if the metric improves, otherwise discard it.
  The README does not discuss overfitting to the evaluation set.

CrocSwap/integer-mult-bounds [V] (https://github.com/CrocSwap/integer-mult-bounds):
  - the metric is exact (the exponent saving kappa, about 0.001635);
  - every win is machine-checked (exact certificates, replayed arithmetic, reproducible make targets);
  - about a dozen stacked checkpoints;
  - failed attempts are archived;
  - conditional claims are labelled as conditional.

The lesson: the loop works when the metric is EXACT, CHEAP and UN-GAMEABLE, and when small
wins add up. It fails when the metric is noisy (a 3rd-decimal AUC inside cross-validation
noise) or when labels are noisy (the agent learns the label noise).

Requirements used to rank candidates:
  (a) frozen, trustworthy labels (synthetic labels are ideal);
  (b) evaluation in seconds to minutes;
  (c) real headroom, well above evaluation noise;
  (d) a locked test set the loop never sees;
  (e) the result means something to astronomers.

======================================================================
1. ML VETTING OF PERIODIC TESS/KEPLER TRANSIT SIGNALS (ExoMiner, Astronet)
======================================================================
State of the art and reported metrics:

* Astronet: Shallue & Vanderburg 2018, AJ 155, 94, arXiv:1712.05044 [R].
  - Kepler DR24 autovetter labels.
  - Test AUC about 0.988.
  - TFRecords from the google-research/exoplanet-ml repo (DR24, 2018) [R].

* Astronet-Triage: Yu et al. 2019, AJ 158, 25, arXiv:1904.02726 [V abstract].
  - TESS QLP; triage test AUC 0.992.

* Astronet-Triage-v2: Tey et al. 2023, AJ 165, 95, arXiv:2301.01371 [V].
  - 24,926 QLP FFI TCEs (S1-39), each labelled by 3-5 human vetters.
  - Test set: recall 99.6% at precision 75.7%.
  - AUPRC 0.965 on held-out EM1 data, +4% over v1.
  - Recovers 3577 of 4140 TOIs.
  - PUBLIC [V]: Zenodo https://zenodo.org/record/7411579 (CC-BY), with
    tces_with_labels_v3.csv (3.1 MB) and LC.tar.gz (6.5 GB of QLP light curves).
  - Code: https://github.com/mdanatg/Astronet-Triage (pinned commit 265366bc).

* ExoMiner: Valizadegan et al. 2022, ApJ 926, 120, arXiv:2111.10009 [V abstract].
  - Kepler DR25: recall 0.936 at precision 0.99, versus 0.763 for the best prior method.
  - Validated 301 planets.

* ExoMiner++: Valizadegan et al. 2025, AJ 170, 287, arXiv:2502.09790 [V HTML].
  - TESS SPOC 2-min, S1-67.
  - Labels: KP/CP/FP/BD from ExoFOP, EB from the Villanova catalogue, NTP from TESS-ExoClass.
    TCE counts: 1835 KP, 1846 CP, 32 BD, 12738 EB, 1702 FP, 39009 NTP.
  - 10-fold cross-validation split BY TARGET STAR, with a 10-model ensemble per fold.
  - Best TESS result (TESS+Kepler training, aggregated):
    precision 0.933, recall 0.951, PR-AUC 0.976, ROC-AUC 0.998, accuracy 0.992.
    Kepler-only: PR-AUC 0.994, ROC-AUC 0.999.
  - PUBLIC [V]: Zenodo https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15466292 holds:
      tfrecords_tess-spoc-2min_s1-s67 (19.0 GB .tar.xz; unnormalised features);
      the labelled TCE catalogue CSV (8.9 MB);
      the unlabelled-TCE scores CSV (36 MB).
    Code: https://github.com/nasa/ExoMiner (NOSA licence; Podman image; preprocessing,
    cross-validation and HPO code).

* ExoMiner++ 2.0 (FFI): Martinho et al. 2026, arXiv:2601.14877 [V abstract]. No metrics in the abstract.

* RAVEN: Hadjigeorghiou et al. 2025, arXiv:2509.17645 [V summary].
  - XGBoost plus GP classifiers trained on 8 synthetic false-positive scenarios injected into TESS.
  - AUC >0.97 on every scenario.
  - On 1361 known TOIs: 97% precision at 66% recall.
  - Limited to P = 0.5-16 d.

* Other work:
  - WATSON-Net reports P = 0.93, R = 0.76 [R].
  - NotPlaNET (arXiv:2405.18278) [V] filters PHT false positives; it flags a median 18% of
    contaminants and keeps essentially all planets. No public data link.

How saturated is this? Very.
- ROC-AUC is 0.995-0.999 and PR-AUC 0.97-0.99.
- The remaining errors are concentrated in the categories where the labels themselves are
  doubtful. The ExoMiner++ paper itself (Sec. VI.7) lists:
    * Villanova EBs labelled by light-curve shape only (the model "misclassified" 15 EBs /
      37 TCEs from that catalogue);
    * ephemeris-matching failures (5 TCEs mislabelled EB, 3 labelled NTP that are really PCs);
    * TCEs at half or double the true period;
    * BDs (recall 0.56).
- On TESS the residual error rate (about 5% of planets missed, about 7% contamination) is
  plausibly comparable to the label-noise rate. A higher PR-AUC would then partly measure
  agreement with label noise.

Could a hobbyist reproduce a baseline in a day?
- Astronet-Triage-v2: probably yes. The code is public, the data is 6.5 GB, the CNN is small,
  and the A4500 is enough.
- ExoMiner++: a reduced single-model baseline from the TFRecords is a 1-2 day job. That
  assumes the TF/Keras environment cooperates on Windows; use WSL or Podman.
  The paper's number is a 10-fold x 10-model ensemble trained jointly with Kepler, so a 5-minute
  experiment cannot reproduce it; you would compare against your own weakened baseline.

Is beating them plausible? BLUNT ANSWER: NO, not in any way that would be publishable.
  1. The headline metrics are saturated, so gains would be in the 3rd decimal, inside the
     fold-to-fold scatter.
  2. Label noise is the binding constraint. An overnight hill-climb on PR-AUC would partly
     learn to reproduce Villanova and ExoFOP labelling errors (Goodhart).
  3. The labels come from ExoFOP dispositions that keep changing. "Beating" a 2025 number on
     2026 labels is not apples to apples.
  4. Nobody needs a fourth-decimal gain. The useful work (new inputs such as difference
     images, attitude data, Kepler transfer) has already been done by a NASA team.
The one partial exception is Astronet-Triage-v2 precision at 99% recall. 75.7% precision
leaves real headroom, and the labels are human consensus. But QLP owns that pipeline, and
a better triage CNN without adoption is a weak paper.

2. ML OR AUTOMATED VETTING AND DETECTION OF SINGLE-TRANSIT EVENTS
======================================================================
Prior work:

- MonoTools (Osborn): https://github.com/hposborn/MonoTools. Alias and period probabilities
  for mono- and duotransits; search plus a vetting heuristic, not ML [R].
- Single-transit catalogues and searches [R]:
    Foreman-Mackey et al. 2016, AJ 152, 206 (arXiv:1607.08237), Kepler long-period search;
    Uehara et al. 2016 (arXiv:1602.07848), Kepler single transits;
    Kawahara & Masuda 2019 (arXiv:1904.04980), Kepler single and double transits;
    Osborn et al. 2016 and LaCourse & Jacobs 2018, K2 single transits;
    Eisner et al. 2021 (arXiv:2011.13944), Planet Hunters TESS long-period candidates;
    Montalto et al. 2020 (DIAmante);
    Gill et al. 2020 (NGTS recovery of TESS singles);
    Hawthorn et al. 2024 (arXiv:2310.17268), duotransits.
- ML specifically for single events:
    Malik et al. 2025, AJ, DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/add46d, arXiv:2211.06903 [V].
      1D CNN trained on PHT volunteer scores; +20% PR-AUC over volunteers on TOI recovery.
      Training on volunteer labels beat training on synthetic injections.
    Hansen & Dittmann 2024, AJ 168, 291, arXiv:2403.03427 [V].
      CNN ensemble with Kepler spacecraft diagnostics; >80% single-transit recovery at
      P = 50-800 d. Follow-up: arXiv:2608.23425 (AJ 172, 164, Aug 2026), 4 new long-period
      Kepler candidates [V].
    Salinas et al. 2025, MNRAS 538, 2031, arXiv:2502.07542 [V].
      Transformer on TESS FFI S1-26, no phase folding; 214 candidates, 88 of them single-transit.
      (Salinas is also on our CTOI exclusion list; they are active here.)
    ExoVeil, Priyanshu 2026, arXiv:2606.02778 [V abstract].
      Transformer flux predictor plus matched filter plus XGBoost; AUC 0.938 on DR25;
      32% recovery of 1000-ppm single transits. Single author; transfer claims are weakly
      supported per a Pith review.
    PLATO-field TESS monotransits: arXiv:2401.10072 [V]. 48 events, 10 removed in vetting.
- NotPlaNET (arXiv:2405.18278) states that human vetting still clearly beats models on
  long-period, single-transit and aperiodic events [V].

How many REAL labelled single events exist? Few. Local probe on data/raw/exofop_toi.csv
(2026-10-11; script in scratchpad, not committed):
  - TOIs whose comments flag single, duo or mono, or that have no period: 236.
  - Their TFOPWG dispositions:
      PC 152, APC 13, CP 23, KP 13, FP 25, FA 10.
  - That gives about 36 planets versus about 35 false positives or false alarms with
    a real outcome.
  - CTOIs: about 969 single-like rows, essentially all user disposition PC with no TFOPWG outcome.
  - Our own singles/results/final_classification.csv (306 stars) labels the outcome of
    only about 60 events (EB-on-target 21, off-target EB 18, inconsistent 23, a handful periodic).
    Those labels come from our own pipeline, so they are circular as training labels.
  - Kepler and K2 single-event catalogues add perhaps 100-300 events, most still unresolved [R].
  Conclusion: real labels are enough for a SANITY CHECK set (about 70 events), not for
  training or a frozen benchmark metric.

Can injection provide training and test data? Yes. It is the standard route (Hansen &
Dittmann; Foreman-Mackey 2016). Planet injections are easy: batman into real PDCSAP or
QLP light curves at random times. The false-positive side is the hard part:
  - eclipsing-binary single eclipses: transplant real eclipses from the Prša 2022 TESS EB
    catalogue (data/raw/tess_eb_prsa2022.tsv is already local) or simulate them with ellc;
  - blends: dilute EB eclipses;
  - systematics: these come for free in the null light curves, e.g. momentum dumps,
    scattered light, sector edges.
Malik et al. found that synthetic training generalises worse than volunteer labels. That
is a real warning about injection realism.

What would a fair frozen test set be?
  - Split by TIC, never by event.
  - Use a fixed injection distribution: depth, duration, impact parameter and time, drawn
    from TOI-like priors.
  - Use a locked seed and a separate locked test star list.
  - Add an external sanity set: the ~70 real ExoFOP single-event outcomes, reported but
    never optimised on.
  - Metric: see Section 3.

Is it a genuine gap? PARTLY.
- Single-event detection with ML is now an active area (Malik, Hansen & Dittmann, Salinas,
  ExoVeil). It is not empty.
- What does NOT exist: a public, frozen, injection-based TESS single-event benchmark with an
  exact metric and published baselines. Every paper reports its own incomparable numbers.
  A benchmark and a strong baseline are a legitimate, citable contribution.
- Beating Malik or Salinas head-to-head is not possible: their test sets are not released
  in comparable form [R; I found no data link for either].

3. INJECTION-RECOVERY TUNING OF A SEARCH OR VETTING PIPELINE
======================================================================
Precedents (this is a well-trodden idea, which makes it respectable):
- Kepler DR25 Robovetter: Thompson et al. 2018, ApJS 235, 38, arXiv:1710.06758 [V summary].
  Its pass/fail thresholds were hand-tuned to maximise completeness on injected TCEs and
  effectiveness on inverted and scrambled light curves. This is literally a manual
  autoresearch loop.
  - For P < 100 d: completeness >85% and reliability >98%.
  - In the hard box (P = 100-500 d, MES <= 10): completeness about 77% and reliability about
    45% with no score cut [V, NASA Exoplanet Archive KOI companion page].
  - Reliability is the known weak point for eta-Earth (Bryson et al. 2020, arXiv:2006.15719 [R]).
  - Every input metric and output disposition for obs, inv, scr1-3, inj1 (on-target planets),
    inj2 (off-target planets) and inj3 (EBs) is public in https://github.com/nasa/kepler-robovetter
    [V; about 110 MB total, 10 tar.gz input files and matching outputs].
- Wotan: Hippke et al. 2019, AJ 158, 143, arXiv:1906.00966 [V]. Compared detrending
  methods by injection-recovery. The biweight wins, recovering 99% of the shallowest Kepler planets.
- TLS versus BLS: Hippke & Heller 2019, A&A 623, A39, arXiv:1901.02015 [R].
- Christiansen et al. 2016, ApJ 828, 99, Kepler pipeline completeness [R].
- Agent-driven science entries already exist: the FAIR Universe weak-lensing challenge at
  NeurIPS 2025 had a "fully agent-driven" entry using CMBAgent
  (https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/136457) [V].

Metric design that resists gaming:
  1. The primary scalar is recall at a FIXED false-alarm budget:
       recall(injected signals) at FAR <= k per 1000 null star-sectors (or, for vetting,
       completeness at fixed effectiveness).
     The threshold is set on the negatives inside the evaluation, never by the agent.
  2. Negatives must include real nulls (light curves with nothing injected), inverted and
     scrambled data, and astrophysical false positives (injected EB eclipses or blends).
     Otherwise the agent wins by "detect anything box-shaped".
  3. Restrict the evaluation to a stated parameter box, e.g. depth 0.5-5 ppt and
     SNR 7-15, so gains cannot come from easy signals.
  4. Split by star, in three ways:
     - dev set: about 60% of stars, which the loop sees;
     - validation set: about 20% of stars, which scores the keep/discard decision; the
       agent sees only the scalar;
     - locked test set: about 20% of stars, evaluated once at the end, by a human.
  5. Freeze the evaluation harness:
     - prepare.py is read-only;
     - its hash is checked at the start of every run;
     - the agent cannot read labels or the injection table;
     - injection parameters live outside the agent's file tree.
  6. Score with a bootstrap. Accept a change only if the gain exceeds about 2 sigma of
     the bootstrap over stars, which stops random-walk acceptance of noise.
  7. Log every experiment, including failures, as the integer-mult repo does.
  8. Make realism attacks hard. Inject into light curves BEFORE detrending, inside the
     frozen harness. Use the real cadence, gaps and quality flags. Randomise the injection
     shape (limb darkening, impact parameter) so the agent cannot key on a template.

======================================================================
4. OTHER ASTRONOMY PROBLEMS (benchmark, best score, data, fit)
======================================================================
a) PLAsTiCC (Kaggle 2018). Results paper: Hložek et al. 2020/2023, arXiv:2012.12392 [V].
   - Winner Kyle Boone (avocado), weighted log-loss 0.68503 private (public 0.6706).
     2nd 0.69933, 3rd 0.70016.
   - The paper notes the competition "remains live", so late submissions are scored.
   - Data: Kaggle, plus the unblinded set on Zenodo (record 2539456 [R]); test set about
     3.5M objects, tens of GB [R].
   - Fit: an exact metric with a public leaderboard, but it is saturated (8 years of
     follow-up papers) and the metric is gameable through the "class 99 / other" weighting.
     A full test evaluation does not fit in 5 minutes, so you would need a frozen subsample.
     Low novelty. A good autoresearch demo, a poor paper.
   - ELAsTiCC (the DESC successor) has no single frozen leaderboard [R]. Poor fit.

b) Photometric redshifts (e.g. Pasquet et al. 2019 SDSS CNN, sigma_MAD about 0.009 [R];
   LSST DESC PZ DC1, Schmidt et al. 2020, arXiv:2001.03621 [R]).
   - Saturated and crowded.
   - Metrics (sigma_MAD, outlier fraction, PIT/CRPS) are easy to trade against each other.
   - No single frozen benchmark that people agree on. Poor fit.

c) Gaia XP stellar parameters (Andrae, Rix & Chandra 2023, arXiv:2302.02611; Zhang, Green &
   Rix 2023, arXiv:2303.02331) [R].
   - Data is public and large (220M spectra; the APOGEE crossmatch is about 0.5M stars).
   - The labels are APOGEE's own systematics, and the field is crowded.
   - Fits 5-minute GBDT/MLP runs well, but gains in the 3rd decimal of [M/H] scatter are
     not news. Moderate fit, low novelty.

d) Ariel Data Challenge 2024/2025 (Kaggle/NeurIPS; e.g. arXiv:2505.08940) [V partial].
   - Exoplanet-adjacent: extract transmission spectra plus uncertainties from simulated
     Ariel focal-plane data.
   - Exact metric (Gaussian log-likelihood score), but 1000+ teams.
   - Data is very large [R: hundreds of GB].
   - Late-submission status unknown; check on Kaggle.
   - Good autoresearch exercise, almost no publication value.

e) FAIR Universe weak-lensing uncertainty challenge (NeurIPS 2025, Codabench 8934) [V].
   - Exact metric, under 10 GB of training data.
   - Already has an agent-driven entry. Not exoplanets. Interesting, off-mission.

f) Period finding for sparse cadence (LSST-like). Precedent: Graham et al. 2013 comparison
   of period finders, arXiv:1307.2209 [R].
   - The metric is exact: the fraction of injected periods recovered within 1%, or
     alias-aware.
   - Data is self-generated: real or simulated variables resampled on Rubin OpSim cadences.
   - No accepted frozen benchmark, so you would build it, as in 2/3. Moderate novelty.
   - Fits 5-minute GPU (cuvarbase) or CPU runs.

g) Duo-transit alias resolution (our own domain).
   - The metric is exact: the rank of the true alias, or log-score, on injected duos in
     real TESS window functions.
   - It is narrow, and MonoTools is the established tool.
   - Most of the alias "algorithm" is deterministic geometry (we already do it), so there is
     little for a hill-climber to improve beyond the likelihood weighting.
   - Low headroom.

h) Kepler DR25 simulated-TCE vetting: see Section 3 and SHORTLIST #1. This is the one
   Section-4-style benchmark that is public, frozen, synthetically labelled and on-mission.

5. RANKED SHORTLIST (TOP 3)
======================================================================
#1  Kepler DR25 "long-period, low-MES" vetter on the Robovetter simulated sets
  Task:
    - Learn a vetter from the published Robovetter input metrics, optionally adding DV
      quantities.
    - Positives: inj1 on-target planet injections.
    - Negatives: inv + scr1-3 (instrumental and noise false alarms) and inj2/inj3
      (off-target and EB astrophysical false positives).
  Metric:
    - Completeness on inj1 at FIXED effectiveness, e.g. matching the Robovetter's own inv+scr
      false-alarm rate, inside P = 100-500 d, MES <= 10 (or 7.1-12).
    - Split by KIC.
    - The locked test set is one KIC partition; scr3 can be held out entirely as a
      second lock.
    - Report the equivalent reliability on obs TCEs using the Thompson/Bryson formula as a
      secondary number.
  Baseline: the DR25 Robovetter itself.
    - Its dispositions for every simulated TCE are in the *_output.txt files.
    - Published box numbers: completeness about 77%, reliability about 45%.
    - ExoMiner (arXiv:2111.10009) also scored inv/scr. Compare to it as a stretch goal.
  Data: https://github.com/nasa/kepler-robovetter, about 110 MB total, tabular.
  Compute:
    - Each experiment is seconds to 2 minutes of CPU (XGBoost/LightGBM or a small MLP), so
      hundreds per night.
    - Harness build: 1-2 days.
  Novelty and publishability:
    - Moderate. A clean "+X% completeness at equal effectiveness in the eta-Earth-relevant
      box, held-out-star test" is an RNAAS. The resulting reliability numbers are directly
      usable by occurrence-rate people.
    - Not glamorous: Kepler is closed, and Bryson et al. and ExoMiner have worked nearby.
  Main risks:
    - inv/scr are imperfect stand-ins for real false alarms. A gain could come from
      simulation quirks, e.g. scrambling-specific artefacts.
      Guard: hold out scr3 and inv separately and require gains on both.
    - Robovetter metrics are hand-engineered and were tuned on these very sets, so the
      headroom may be smaller than the 45% reliability suggests.
    - The agent could exploit features that identify which set a TCE came from (e.g.
      quarter patterns). Audit the feature list.
  Why #1: it is the closest analogue to the integer-mult setting. The labels are exact
  (synthetic), evaluation is seconds, the baseline is frozen and public, and it is on-mission.

#2  TESS single-event detection and vetting benchmark (injection-based), built from our data
  Task:
    - Given a real TESS light curve (SPOC 2-min or our QLP/TESScut FFI), output a
      single-event score.
    - Positives: batman single transits injected inside the frozen harness.
    - Negatives:
        real nulls;
        injected single EB eclipses (shapes drawn from Prša 2022);
        blended EBs;
        native systematics.
  Metric:
    - Recall of injected planets at FAR <= 1 per 1000 star-sectors.
    - Box: depth 1-10 ppt, duration 2-15 h, SNR 7-20.
    - Star-split dev/val/locked-test as in Section 3.
    - External sanity set: the ~36 CP/KP versus ~35 FP/FA real ExoFOP single-event outcomes
      (local probe). Report on it, never optimise on it.
  Baseline:
    - Ours: a wotan biweight plus box/matched-filter single-event search, which is roughly
      what the singles pipeline does.
    - Literature anchors, not directly comparable: Hansen & Dittmann 2024 (>80% for Kepler
      P = 50-800 d), ExoVeil (32% at 1000 ppm), Malik 2025, Salinas 2025.
  Data:
    - Already local: 3.3 GB of light curves and 18 GB of TESScut in data/raw.
    - A real benchmark needs about 5-10k stars x 1-3 sectors of SPOC 2-min, about 20-40 GB
      from MAST, plus the Prša EB table (already local).
  Compute:
    - The evaluation must run in under 5 minutes, so precompute and cache the light curves,
      and evaluate on about 2-3k star-sectors with about 10k injections. A GPU matched filter
      or a small 1D CNN fits easily on the A4500.
    - Harness build: about 1-2 weeks of careful human-plus-agent work.
  Novelty and publishability:
    - Highest of the three. There is no public frozen TESS single-event benchmark.
    - A benchmark, an optimised open-source search and a locked-test result make an RNAAS
      first, and could grow to AJ/PASP.
    - It feeds straight back into the singles and K2 work.
  Main risks:
    - Injection realism. Malik et al. found that synthetic training generalises worse than
      real labels.
    - EB and blend simulation fidelity.
    - The benchmark design is itself the contribution, so referees will poke at it.
    - Building it is the hard part. Autoresearch only helps once it is frozen.

#3  Astronet-Triage-v2 precision at fixed 99% recall (TESS QLP FFI triage)
  Metric:
    - Precision at recall >= 0.99 on transiting/eclipsing ("E") versus the rest.
    - Use the paper's train/val/test split, or star-grouped folds.
    - Hold out the EM1 subset as the lock (that is how the paper scored AUPRC 0.965).
  Baseline: Tey et al. 2023, 75.7% precision at 99.6% recall; AUPRC 0.965 on EM1.
  Data: Zenodo 7411579 (6.5 GB of light curves, 3.1 MB of labels, CC-BY); code at
    github.com/mdanatg/Astronet-Triage.
  Compute: small CNN, about 25k examples, so 2-5 minutes per run on the A4500.
    Reproducing the baseline takes about 1 day.
  Novelty and publishability:
    - Low to moderate. Human-consensus labels and a real precision gap, but the field is
      mature, QLP owns the pipeline, and gains need adoption to matter.
    - At best an RNAAS or a pull request offered to the QLP team.
  Main risks:
    - Label noise among the 5 classes.
    - The split must be by TIC.
    - Precision at 99% recall is a tail metric, so it is high-variance. Bootstrap it.

NOT RECOMMENDED: beating ExoMiner++ (Zenodo 15466292, PR-AUC 0.976 on TESS).
  - The metrics are saturated.
  - The residual errors are label noise (Villanova shape-only EBs, ephemeris mismatches).
  - The reference number comes from a 10x10 ensemble with Kepler transfer.
  - Overnight hill-climbing would fit noise.
  Reproducing a reduced version is fine as a learning exercise, not as a claim.

======================================================================
6. RECOMMENDATION FOR THE FIRST AUTORESEARCH LOOP
======================================================================
Start with #1, the Kepler DR25 simulated-TCE vetter in the long-period, low-MES box.
- It is the only candidate that meets all five requirements on day one: exact synthetic
  labels, a public frozen baseline (the Robovetter outputs), about 110 MB of tabular data,
  second-scale evaluations, and a scientifically meaningful scalar (completeness at fixed
  effectiveness, which maps to the reliability that eta-Earth estimates need).
- It also tests whether the loop, the guardrails and Jon's workflow behave before he spends
  weeks building a bespoke benchmark.

Concretely:
  1. prepare.py (frozen):
     - parse the 10 input/output files;
     - KIC-grouped split: 60% dev, 20% val, 20% locked; scr3 held out entirely;
     - compute the Robovetter baseline in the box;
     - define the fixed-effectiveness threshold rule;
     - bootstrap the confidence interval.
  2. train.py (the agent edits only this): feature engineering, model choice, ensembling.
     Each run is capped at 5 minutes.
  3. Keep a change only if validation completeness at fixed effectiveness rises by more
     than 2 bootstrap sigma. Log every run.
  4. Once at the end: evaluate on the locked partition and on scr3 / inv separately.
     If the gain holds on both, write the RNAAS.

Then move to #2, the TESS single-event benchmark, as the real research product. It reuses
the same harness pattern, and it is where the novelty is and where Jon's existing data and
pipeline pay off.

Blunt bottom line:
- Do not aim the loop at ExoMiner or Astronet headline metrics. They are saturated and
  limited by label noise; a "win" there would be fitting noise.
- The defensible wins are where labels are synthetic and exact (DR25 inj/inv/scr, or our
  own frozen injection harness), and where the evaluation box is the hard regime: long
  period, low SNR, single events.

Probe used: scratchpad/probe_singles.py on data/raw/exofop_toi.csv and exofop_ctoi.csv
(read-only). No repo files were modified other than this one.
