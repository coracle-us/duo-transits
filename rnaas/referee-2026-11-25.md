# Referee read of the four open RNAAS notes (2026-11-25)

Read-only. No note was edited, uploaded or withdrawn, and nobody was contacted.

**The test applied.** AAS84038 was rejected because its derived quantity did not support its conclusion and its
parameter choices were not justified. Each note below is read as a skeptical RNAAS referee would read it, with
that test first: does the main claim follow from what is shown, is a plausible alternative left open, are the
thresholds justified, does the abstract say more than the body, and would the result stand on its own.

**What was read.** The exact versions AAS holds, as listed in `rnaas/audit-2026-11-25.md`:

| Note | Source read |
|---|---|
| AAS84033 | `k2singles/rnaas/rnaas.tex` at a2f5aaf |
| AAS84040 | `boyajian-companion/rnaas/rnaas.tex` at tag `aas84040-submitted` |
| AAS84041 | `eb_depth_evolution/rnaas/rnaas.tex` at 4438ecf |
| AAS84043 | `be-star-pulsations/rnaas/ms.tex` at tag `aas84043-submitted` |

For each note I also read its `numbers.md` at the same commit, and for AAS84040 the scan script and logs.

**Limits of this read.**
- No analysis was rerun. The checks are of logic, of the text against the provenance files, and a few
  hand calculations (marked "my estimate").
- I did not see the editor's rejection letter for AAS84038. The test above is Jon's summary of it.
- The verdicts are my judgement of a moderated journal's likely response, not predictions with a known error rate.
- The editor has noted how many notes have been sent. That raises the bar for every note below, and it makes
  overlap between notes (AAS84033 and AAS84041) more costly than it would otherwise be.

## Ranking, most at risk first

1. **AAS84033 (K2 periods): likely reject.** The check named in the title fails for the two results the abstract
   leads with.
2. **AAS84043 (Be stars): likely minor revision, with a real chance of rejection.** The control band fails and
   the abstract does not say so.
3. **AAS84040 (Boyajian's star): likely minor revision.** The logic holds, but the thresholds are unexplained
   and the stated validation leaves something out.
4. **AAS84041 (EB): likely minor revision, the closest to an accept.** The observation is solid. The
   third-body numbers repeat the AAS84038 fault on a small scale, and the PDF AAS holds has a broken first page.

## AAS84033: K2 single-transit periods

**Verdict: likely reject.** This is the note most likely to fail the AAS84038 test.

**Context.** It is the revision of AAS82225, which the editor declined on 2026-11-15 while asking for two
things: a thorough literature search and a blind search. He will read the revision against those two requests.

### Does the main claim follow?

Partly. The note makes about ten claims in 1,300 words. The eclipsing-binary periods are well supported. The two
planet-candidate periods, which the abstract features, are not supported by the check the title advertises.

### The objections he is most likely to raise

**1. The blind search does not support the headline results.**
- The title says the periods are "Checked by a Blind Search". The search misses both planet candidates.
- For TOI-5560.01 the body says the linked periods are "only at threshold support, among 16 periods and below
  the star's inverted control: not a detection". The inverted (noise) light curve gives more support than the
  real one (`numbers.md`: support 4, own control 6).
- The abstract still states, as a result, that "TOI-5560.01 narrows from ≤702 d to 140.494 or 70.247 d,
  excluding four SPOC threshold-crossing events". That claim rests on the directed analysis alone: four dips on
  one linear ephemeris. No chance-alignment probability is given for the two K2 dips, which have SNR 9 to 32.
- This is the AAS84038 pattern: the derived check does not support the conclusion drawn.
- "11 of the 15 periods among its allowed periods" is also a weak test. The blind search allows 2 to 40 periods
  per star (`numbers.md`), and the note does not say so.

**2. The search was changed after the results were seen, and its thresholds are not explained.**
- Methods: "The threshold followed a one-star test; a coincidence test ... (replacing the planned rule) and a
  linked-event test ... came after the full run."
- The thresholds are SNR 53 (K2) and 60 (TESS). The note does not say why 0.05 false events per star was chosen,
  or that the control is inflated by glints and bad cadences (the response letter says this; the note does not).
  A threshold that high can confirm only deep eclipses, so the search could never have tested the planet
  candidates.
- Other unexplained choices: first stage SNR ≥ 8, dips of 1 to 48 h, BLS to 100 d, the 99th percentile.

**3. The novelty claim rests on a search whose measured recall is one third.**
- He asked for a thorough literature search. The note reports that ADS and SIMBAD found 3 of 9 known pairs, that
  the VizieR recall cannot be measured, that 13 of 93 catalogues were missing, and that the search radius missed
  a known entry.
- Reporting the limits is honest. But "finds no prior orbital period for six" then carries little weight, and
  the response letter goes further than the note ("6 of the 15 periods are new").

### Other points

- **Abstract without support in the body.** The last abstract sentence, on EPIC 202126877 deepening from 6% to
  36%, has no body text behind it (audit check 8). It is also the headline result of AAS84041. Two notes in the
  queue reporting the same finding is the kind of thing an editor counting submissions will notice.
- **Aliases.** Seven of the 15 "periods" are sets of two to five values. "I report periods for 15 of them" reads
  as 15 solved periods.
- **Formal errors.** Uncertainties such as 49.250131(13) d come from linear fits to two or three events. The
  note does not say how other submultiples were excluded for the single-valued rows.
- **Would it stand alone?** As a table of eclipsing-binary periods, yes. As a note about two planet candidates
  checked by a blind search, no.

### Can it be fixed at RNAAS length?

Only by claiming less.
- Recast it as a table of eclipsing-binary periods from K2 and TESS, with the two planet candidates given as
  "proposed periods, not confirmed by the blind search".
- Change the title so it does not promise a check that fails for the featured results.
- Drop the EPIC 202126877 sentence from the abstract and point to AAS84041.
- Add the chance-alignment probability for TOI-5560.01 if it can be computed. If it comes out strong, it is the
  one number that would rescue that claim.
- The first transit that separates 70.25 d from 140.49 d is predicted for 2027 February 28. A note after that
  date would be much stronger than this one.

## AAS84043: Be-star variability against WISE disk excess

**Verdict: likely minor revision, with a real chance of rejection** if he reads the control paragraph closely.

### Does the main claim follow?

The title claim (more variable in TESS when the W1 excess is larger) is supported as a population statistic. The
headline number in the abstract is not the number the note's own control supports.

### The objections he is most likely to raise

**1. The control fails, and the abstract leaves it out.**
- The white-noise band (15 to 24 c/d) was defined in Methods as "a control". It also rises: β = 0.33.
- The abstract reports "At higher frequencies the rise is about 3%" as a finding. That is the same slope
  (0.31 to 0.37) the body treats as a control that did not stay flat.
- The body's explanation ("the band appears to carry the tail of the stochastic signal") is made after the fact
  and is not tested independently.
- Divided by the control, the slopes fall from 0.74–0.84 to 0.41–0.51, and they are "not significantly" positive
  in SPOC stars alone (p ≥ 0.13). SPOC is the best photometry in the sample.
- So the defensible headline is about 4 to 5% per 0.1 mag above the control, not confirmed in SPOC alone. The
  abstract gives 8% and neither caveat. This is the AAS84038 pattern in a milder form.

**2. A simpler reading is not ruled out, and the note half-concedes it.**
- The Discussion says the low-frequency band and flicker "also include the days-long brightening of outbursts".
  Outbursts are what build the disk. The result may therefore restate that disk-building events are visible in
  optical photometry, which is known.
- 70% of the stars (755 of 1,084) use QLP `SAP_FLUX` (`numbers.md`), which is not corrected for systematics,
  with only a 5-day spline. The headline band, 0.25 to 0.8 c/d, is where TESS systematics are worst. The swap
  null and the sector fixed effects answer most of this, but the note never says which QLP flux it used.
- Brightness normalisation (my estimate): the star brightens by k = 0.21 mag in T per mag of W1 excess. In QLP,
  normalised to the catalogue flux, photon noise then rises by about 0.10 per mag, which is a third of the
  control's 0.33. The dilution correction for SPOC assumes the variable light is photospheric, which sits
  awkwardly with the outburst reading above.
- The sample was selected for W1 variability and most stars are unconfirmed. In the 256 with Hα emission the
  slope is lower, 0.58 [0.29, 0.87].

**3. Many choices, few reasons.**
- Unexplained: band edges 0.25–0.8 c/d, 5-day knots, the 250-day gap, the 0.1 mag overshoot, CROWDSAP ≥ 0.9, the
  0.4 mag cut behind "71% of 177".
- Post hoc, and labelled so only in part: the narrow frequency bins, the noisiest-third split.
- The note admits the p-values are uncorrected for the several metrics and samples.
- The many robustness checks help here. This objection alone would not sink the note.

### Other points

- **Length.** The audit puts it 6 to 46 words over the limit by AAS's count, and the figure is 28 pt too tall.
  With the volume comment, a recount is a cheap reason to send it back.
- **Keyword.** "Stellar pulsations" is listed, but the note finds no response of the dominant pulsation.
- **Would it stand alone?** Yes, as a population trend with the corrected headline.

### Can it be fixed at RNAAS length?

Yes, and the fix shortens the note.
- Lead the abstract with the control-divided slope and say the control band also rises.
- State that the trend is not significant in SPOC stars alone after that division.
- Say which flux column each product uses.
- Cut the dominant-frequency paragraph to one sentence. That gives back the words needed and brings the note
  under the limit.

## AAS84040: Boyajian's star, the ~780 d periods and window 4

**Verdict: likely minor revision.**

### Does the main claim follow?

Yes, given one assumption: every transit of the companion is as deep as the one TESS saw (1.1%), and deficits
add. Under that assumption the exclusions are simple and sound.

### The objections he is most likely to raise

**1. The rule's parameters are stated but not justified, and one conclusion turns on them.**
- The rule: 0.8% threshold, three or more cadences, a core of ±0.25 d, quiet level from the 95th percentile
  within ±3 d.
- The note tests robustness only to the core width and to T0. It does not vary the 0.8% threshold or the
  quiet-level window. (The script has a `--mask` option, but I found no run of it among the logs at the tag.)
- The one period SN26 actually ties to the TESS object is 776.1 d. The note calls it "marginally excluded"
  because its core lies "within 0.6–0.8% of quiet", which is against a 0.8% threshold, on the egress of a dip
  where a ±3 d quiet level is hard to define. The range 776.17–776.60 d, starting 0.07 d away, survives.
- So the answer to "is SN26's remnant idea ruled out?" depends on the unexplained threshold. This is the second
  half of the AAS84038 test.

**2. The stated validation leaves out a mismatch.**
- The note says: "As a check, the code recovers MF26's windows 1, 2, 3 and 5 to within ~0.5 d at each edge."
- The same run leaves 33 further ranges between 1025 and 1375 d that MF26 do not list
  (`window4_check_2026-11-24.log`). One of them, 1053.99–1059.14 d, is 5 d wide. `numbers.md` records this. The
  note does not.
- Likely causes are a different treatment of flagged cadences (the scan drops everything with
  `SAP_QUALITY` ≠ 0 and counts it as a gap) or MF26's radial-velocity prior. The note does not say which.
- The check is good to 0.5 d, while two of the four surviving ranges near 780 d are 0.15–0.16 d wide.
- A referee who reran the public code would find the 33 ranges within minutes.

**3. Window 4 rests on one dip and on constant depth, but the title says "rules out".**
- The exclusion: the predicted transit falls on a known dip only 0.44% deep, and a 1.1% transit cannot make a
  shallower dip. That logic is right.
- It needs the transit depth to be constant. The body says so ("unless the transit is much shallower than in
  TESS"). The title and abstract do not.
- Constant depth is a real question for this star. Zhu & Liu (2026), cited in the note, fit the same transit with
  a ringed planet, and rings can change projected depth. The Kepler and TESS bandpasses also differ.
- The same assumption underlies every exclusion near 780 d, and there it is not stated at all.
- The coincidence with the dip is not evidence for window 4 either: MF26's window exists only because that dip
  let the epoch pass their 0.1% rule. One sentence saying so would close the point.

### Other points

- **Is the comparison fair to SN26?** SN26's 781.6 and 800.9 d are recurrence periods of the dust, not a claimed
  period of the companion. Excluding them as companion periods tests something SN26 only hinted at. And since
  Kepler observed about 90% of the time, "rules out most ~780-day periods" is close to automatic.
- **"Kepler alone excludes only P < 776 d."** The logs at the tag scan 770–845 d and 1025–1375 d. I found no
  scan below 770 d or between 845 and 1025 d, so this sentence is not shown by the saved outputs.
- **The ground-based section** is a null result that takes a quarter of the note, and its Δχ² > 25 threshold is
  unexplained. It is harmless, and it is the place to find words.
- **Would it stand alone?** Yes. Window 4 is the useful result. The rest is modest.

### Can it be fixed at RNAAS length?

Yes.
- One sentence giving the reason for 0.8% (for example: the transit depth minus three times the local scatter)
  and the result at 0.6% and 1.0%.
- One sentence disclosing the 33 extra ranges and the likely reason.
- "Excluded if the transit depth is constant" in the abstract, and a softer title verb.
- Pay for these by shortening the ground-based section.

## AAS84041: EB EPIC 202126877, eclipses switching on

**Verdict: likely minor revision, the closest of the four to an accept.** The PDF must be fixed first.

### Does the main claim follow?

The observational claim does, and it does not depend on the model: no eclipse above 4.5% in 2004–2008, a 6.2%
primary with no secondary in 2014, then 20.6%, 27.4% and 35.6%, with the secondary appearing and growing.

### Apsidal motion against inclination change

This alternative does not survive, but the note does not make the argument, and it should.
- Apsidal motion moves the two impact parameters in opposite directions. Both eclipses deepen here, so cos i
  must be falling (my reading of the geometry: the impact parameters scale as cos i/(1 ± e sin ω)).
- The size also rules it out. cos i falls by a factor of about 3.6 between 85.7° and 88.8°, while the timing
  shows e cos ω changing by only about 0.001 per orbit (my estimate from 26 min).
- What a referee will object to instead: the note dismisses relativistic, tidal and rotational apsidal motion
  only. The third body it argues for would drive apsidal motion on about the same timescale as the nodal
  precession. The fit holds e sin ω fixed across all seasons while the note itself reports e cos ω changing
  within months. So the inclination values are model-dependent by an amount the note does not quantify beyond
  "0.1–0.4°".

### The objections he is most likely to raise

**1. The third-body numbers are derived quantities that are not shown.** This is the AAS84038 fault in small.
- "An order-of-magnitude precession estimate gives an outer period of ~100–600 d": no formula, no inputs.
- "Gaia DR3 ... implying a tertiary below ~0.3 M☉ or an outer period near 100 d": not derived in the note. The
  provenance gives one data point, 0.5 M☉ at 200 d → 0.18 mas. A wobble that small would not clearly raise
  RUWE for a G = 11 star (my estimate), so the stated limit is soft.
- An outer period near 100 d is 4.6 times the inner period, which is at the edge of dynamical stability for an
  inner eccentricity of 0.35 (my estimate from the usual stability criterion). The note does not mention it.
- The body says the effects "require a close third body". The abstract says they "point to" one. No third body
  is detected. "Point to" is the defensible wording, and the title's "Rapid Inclination Change" states the
  model's reading as fact.

**2. The rate change and the dated predictions rest on two weak end points and straight-line extrapolation.**
- The 2014 inclination comes from one K2 Campaign 0 eclipse. The 2026 inclination comes from one eclipse whose
  egress fell in a gap, in a 7-day pointing. The two analyses differ by 0.3° there, and the note says so.
- The model systematics (0.1–0.4°, "mostly in one direction") are as large as the difference between the two
  rates over four years.
- The conclusion that the rate changed does survive: a constant TESS-era rate predicts a 1% primary in 2014
  against 6.2% observed, and dilution can only make the observed depth too small.
- But the onset date (2007–2011) extends the early rate backwards in a straight line, and totality (2027–2031)
  extends the late rate forwards, in a note whose own finding is that the rate is not constant. Nodal precession
  is not linear in time, and the inclination could turn around.

**3. Overlap with AAS84033.**
- The deepening is also in the abstract of AAS84033, submitted the same day.
- The Data section's "As in the companion note, the primary is 6.2% ... 35.6%" is now false, because the K2
  note no longer gives those depths (audit check 8).
- Given the volume comment, he may ask why this is two notes.

### Other points

- **Broken first page.** In the PDF AAS holds, the opening paragraph is typeset over the title block (audit
  check 10). This alone is likely to bring the note back.
- **Ground limits.** The phase-offset window (−0.6 to +0.3 d) is not explained, and the note does not say how
  many predicted eclipses SuperWASP and KELT actually covered.
- **Would it stand alone?** Yes. It is the one note of the four whose core is a clear new observation with
  dated, testable predictions.

### Can it be fixed at RNAAS length?

Yes. Most fixes remove words.
- Restore `\section{Introduction}` and drop "As in the companion note," (audit fix 1).
- Cut the outer-period and tertiary-mass numbers, or give the formula and inputs in one sentence each.
- Add one sentence on why apsidal motion cannot produce the deepening (both eclipses deepen).
- Say the onset and totality dates are linear extrapolations and could be wrong if the precession turns.
- Consider a title that states the observation ("Eclipses Switching On in ...") without the model's reading.

## What the four share

These are the patterns that drew the AAS84038 rejection, and they recur:

- **Thresholds given as numbers with no reason.** SNR 53 and 60 (AAS84033); 0.8% and ±3 d (AAS84040); Δχ² > 25
  (AAS84040); 0.25–0.8 c/d and 0.4 mag (AAS84043); −0.6 to +0.3 d (AAS84041). One clause of reasoning each
  would remove the objection.
- **Abstracts that are firmer than the body.** TOI-5560.01 "narrows" (AAS84033); 8% without the control
  (AAS84043); window 4 "excluded" without the depth assumption (AAS84040); the third body (AAS84041).
- **Derived quantities stated without the derivation.** The tertiary's period and mass limit (AAS84041) are the
  closest match to what was rejected.
- **A full paper's worth of claims in 1,300 words.** AAS84033 and AAS84043 most of all. Each would be safer
  with one claim and its strongest check.
- **Disclosed post hoc steps.** Disclosure is right, but a reader sees a test that was adjusted after the
  result (AAS84033, AAS84043).
