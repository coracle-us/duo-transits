\title{Revisiting Stale TESS Single-transit Community Candidates: Mostly Eclipsing Binaries, Two New Duotransits}

\author[orcid=0009-0000-7524-4470]{Jon McBee}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, USA}
\email{jon.mcbee@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
Community TESS Objects of Interest (CTOIs) flagged as single transits are rarely revisited.
We re-examined 306 such stars against all TESS data through Sector 108. We used a validated
repeat search, our own photometry for sectors not yet processed by the TESS pipelines,
difference-image localization and Gaia DR3 binarity diagnostics. Of 272 stars whose catalogued
event we recover, 79 show repeat events. Of these, 21 are Gaia-flagged binaries (three Gaia
orbits reproduce our periods), 18 are blends, 23 fit no single period, and 17 needed vetting by
hand. CTOI 147035057.01 has a fixed 37.315\,d period, but a grazing eclipse on an RV-variable
host, so its companion is probably stellar. Two on-target duotransits survive. In TIC 159614322,
a partial third transit leaves $P=89.965$ or $179.930$\,d; TIC 229055790 is the other. For 193
candidates that remain single, a median 52\% of log-period space (5--1500\,d) is now excluded.
\end{abstract}

\section{Sample}

ExoFOP lists hundreds of CTOIs uploaded as single transits, with no period, mostly in
2020--2022. We took the 450 open CTOIs flagged as single or duo transits, excluding those of
groups still working their candidates. We then removed 46 with missing parameters, 8 on stars
hosting confirmed planets, 4 on stars with a TOI, and 24 on catalogued TESS eclipsing binaries
\citep{Prsa2022}. This left 368 CTOIs on 359 stars. Of these, 32 already carry a community
period and were not re-analyzed. Some entries are simply stale: CTOI 1042868.01, for example,
is the RV-detected planet HD~88986\,b, whose possible TESS transits were analyzed by
\citet{Heidari2024}.

\section{Methods}

We used SPOC 2-min, TESS-SPOC \citep{Caldwell2020} and QLP \citep{Huang2020} light curves from
MAST. For Sectors $\geq100$ without pipeline products, we performed our own aperture photometry
on TESScut cutouts \citep{Brasseur2019}; it recovers a known eclipse depth to 2\%.

The repeat search slides a box of the catalogued depth $\delta$ and duration $D$ on a $D/4$
grid, with segment-aware local baselines. A detection needs SNR $\geq8$, a depth of
0.5--2$\,\delta$, at least 80\% coverage, and baseline on both sides. Candidate periods come
from linear-ephemeris fits to all events. Any period with a predicted epoch on flat data is
rejected. We validated the method blind, starting from the first transit only, on 26 TESS
duotransit hosts with known periods \citep[including][]{Hawthorn2024}. It kept the true period
in 26/26 and returned it as the unique solution in 19, with no wrong unique answers.

We localized sources with TESScut difference images, measuring offsets from the target's Gaia
DR3 position \citep{Gaia2023}. A source is off-target if it is offset by $\geq6''$ at
$\geq3\sigma$; ``firm'' requires two agreeing images. On known planets the offsets are
1--3$''$. Binarity indicators are Gaia DR3 \texttt{non\_single\_star}, two-body orbits, and RV
variability \citep[$p\leq0.01$, renormalised GOF $>4$;][]{Katz2023}.

\section{Results}

Of the 306 stars, 34 do not show the catalogued event in our reductions, and 193 show no repeat.
The 79 stars with repeats break down as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item 21 are on Gaia-flagged binaries. Three Gaia SB1 orbits reproduce our TESS periods
  (Table~\ref{tab:systems}), so these are eccentric eclipsing binaries.
\item 18 are firmly off-target, i.e.\ blends with neighbors.
\item 23 show repeats that no single period explains.
\item 17 were vetted by hand: 2 survive (below), 3 remain tentative, 5 are possibly off-target,
  and 7 were rejected (inconsistent depths, absent in an independent reduction, or a host too
  large).
\end{itemize}
The 3 tentative cases have a repeat seen so far only in our own Sector~107 photometry.

\textit{CTOI 147035057.01.} Three eclipses (Sectors 7, 33, and 108, the last from our own
photometry) fix $P=37.31497\pm0.00003$\,d; the S108 eclipse rules out every shorter alias. The
eclipse is on target, with no secondary at phase 0.5. A fit with impact parameter and radius
ratio free favours a V-shaped grazing eclipse over a full transit ($\Delta$BIC\,$\approx10$).
The radius ratio is $k=0.34$ (95\%: 0.09--0.75), so $R_2>1.7\,R_{\rm J}$ for the
2.0\,$R_\odot$ host. Gaia DR3 measures an RV amplitude of 50\,km\,s$^{-1}$, which implies
$M_2\gtrsim0.6\,M_\odot$. The companion is probably stellar. A low RUWE (1.12), however, leaves
a near-equal SB2 pair possible, and Gaia DR4 epoch RVs should decide.

\textit{TIC 159614322.} Transits in Sectors 14 and 41 have consistent depths ($\approx3.2$\,ppt,
$\approx9.7$\,h) across products, and they are on target. Flat data leave $P=719.72/n$ with
$n\in\{1,2,4,5,8\}$. Sector 21 then contains an ingress and $\approx9$\,h of transit floor,
with the egress lost in the orbit-1 downlink gap. It appears in TESS-SPOC, QLP, and our own
photometry, with a flat background and no pointing step. A blind re-analysis with independent
code also found it. It selects $n=8$ or $n=4$, giving $P=89.9651\pm0.0006$ or
$179.9302\pm0.0012$\,d with $T_0={\rm BJD_{TDB}}\,2458703.8277\pm0.0041$. These values are fitted
from the two full transits; the partial transit arrives 13--26\,min late, which may reflect a
timing bias from the missing egress. The companion is $\approx0.9\,R_{\rm J}$, and the host is
RV-stable in Gaia DR3. Both periods predict a transit at 2026-12-28 06:42\,UTC ($\pm45$\,min,
errors inflated $\times2.1$). A dedicated sweep of all 272 stars for transits truncated by data
gaps found no other convincing case: gap-edge dips do not outnumber gap-edge brightenings.

\textit{TIC 229055790.} This candidate, uploaded by the Planet Hunters TESS project
\citep[see][]{Eisner2021}, shows transits in
Sectors 1 and 68 with consistent depths, on target, re-detected independently. With 38 allowed
periods $\geq26.7$\,d and $R_p\approx3.8\,R_\oplus$, it is worth monitoring.

\section{Conclusion}

Stale single-transit CTOIs with repeat signals are mostly stellar, and inexpensive Gaia binarity
checks and difference imaging dispose of most of them. The residue is small but useful: two
on-target duotransits with alias lists short enough to target, and a few tentative repeats that
pipeline products will confirm or reject. Code, light-curve products and classifications are at
\url{https://github.com/coracle-us/duo-transits}.
